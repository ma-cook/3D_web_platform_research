# Abstract

As remote and hybrid work becomes the dominant model across office environments, government agencies, and educational institutions, organisations face a persistent challenge: replicating the spatial, collaborative, and cognitive depth of in-person work through digital interfaces. This paper examines a growing body of academic research and market evidence supporting the value of browser-based, non-headset 3D applications and websites in addressing these challenges.

Unlike virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) headset systems, non-VR 3D applications run directly in standard web browsers on desktop computers using technologies such as WebGL and WebGPU, requiring no specialist hardware. This accessibility positions them as practical, scalable solutions for organisations of all sizes and budgets.

Drawing on peer-reviewed studies, government deployment case studies, and education technology research, this paper finds that browser-based 3D environments offer measurable benefits across three key domains: (1) collaborative data comprehension and decision-making in office settings; (2) planning, transparency, and multi-agency coordination in government contexts; and (3) student engagement, knowledge retention, and spatial reasoning in educational institutions. The paper concludes with a framework for practical adoption and notes important caveats where the evidence is still developing.

# 1. Introduction

The global shift toward remote and hybrid work — dramatically accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic — has exposed the limitations of the tools organisations rely upon to keep distributed teams connected and productive. Video conferencing platforms, shared documents, and flat dashboards serve many functions well, but they struggle with a fundamental constraint: they render complex, multi-dimensional information in two dimensions.

Spatial reasoning, collaborative design, architectural understanding, scientific modelling, and data analysis that involves layered or geographic information all benefit from three-dimensional representation. When these tasks are carried out remotely, the cognitive gap between what flat screens can convey and what a physical environment or model would communicate becomes a real productivity and comprehension cost.

For many years, 3D applications were associated with specialist hardware: high-end workstations, VR headsets, or dedicated visualisation suites. This has changed substantially. Browser-based 3D technology — powered by the WebGL standard, now maturing into WebGPU — can deliver interactive, real-time three-dimensional environments to any modern desktop browser with no installation required. This democratisation of 3D means the technology is no longer the exclusive preserve of architects, engineers, or game developers. It is becoming a practical tool for general office workers, policy analysts, and educators.

This paper focuses specifically on non-VR, non-headset 3D applications accessed via desktop computers — a distinction that matters greatly for organisational adoption. Headset-based VR, while impressive, carries significant friction: high hardware cost, physical setup requirements, hygiene concerns with shared devices, motion sickness for some users, and a fundamentally different workflow that sits outside normal office computing. Browser-based 3D applications, by contrast, integrate seamlessly into existing workflows, can be accessed remotely by any member of a distributed team, and require nothing more than a modern browser and a stable internet connection.

This review examines evidence for the value of these technologies across three distinct organisational contexts — commercial office environments, government agencies, and educational institutions — with attention to the specific mechanisms by which 3D environments contribute to better outcomes in remote settings.

# 2. Technology Overview: Browser-Based 3D Without Headsets

Understanding what is meant by non-VR 3D applications requires a brief orientation to the technology landscape.

## 2.1 WebGL and WebGPU

WebGL (Web Graphics Library) is a JavaScript API that enables interactive 3D and 2D graphics to be rendered in any compatible web browser using the GPU, without requiring plugins or additional software. Since its standardisation by the Khronos Group, WebGL has achieved near-universal browser support across desktop platforms, making it the foundational layer for browser-based 3D experiences.

WebGPU, the next-generation successor to WebGL, is now in active rollout across major browsers. It provides significantly improved performance, better GPU utilisation, and a more modern rendering pipeline, enabling richer, more detailed 3D scenes at higher frame rates. The transition to WebGPU further expands what is achievable in a browser-based 3D context, particularly for data-intensive applications such as scientific visualisation and large-scale geographic modelling.

## 2.2 Libraries and Platforms

Built on WebGL and WebGPU, a mature ecosystem of frameworks — most notably Three.js, Babylon.js, and CesiumJS (for geospatial 3D) — allows developers to build sophisticated 3D applications that run directly in browsers. These frameworks abstract the complexity of GPU programming, enabling the creation of interactive 3D dashboards, collaborative design environments, data visualisation tools, and virtual spaces without requiring end-users to install anything.

## 2.3 What "Non-VR 3D" Means in Practice

For the purposes of this paper, non-VR 3D applications are those which:

- Run in a standard desktop web browser or native desktop application without requiring a headset
- Allow users to navigate, rotate, and interact with three-dimensional environments using a mouse, keyboard, or touch interface
- Can be accessed remotely by geographically distributed teams simultaneously
- Include collaborative features such as shared views, annotations, real-time updates, and version history

Examples include browser-based digital twins, 3D data dashboards, interactive architectural models, scientific molecule viewers, collaborative virtual office layouts, and 3D geographic information systems (GIS). These contrast with VR applications in that they do not require headsets, are accessible to all staff on standard hardware, and integrate naturally into existing workflows.

# 3. The Remote Work Challenge

To understand why 3D applications represent a meaningful solution, it is important to first understand the specific dimensions of the remote work challenge they address.

## 3.1 The Flattening of Complex Information

Organisations deal with information that is inherently three-dimensional or spatial in nature: building layouts, infrastructure networks, geographic distributions, scientific structures, and data sets with multiple interacting variables. When this information is rendered in two dimensions on flat screens, nuance is lost, errors can go undetected, and the cognitive effort required to reconstruct spatial relationships increases significantly.

Research in neuroergonomics has demonstrated that three-dimensional visual representation reduces cognitive load in spatial reasoning tasks. A study published in 2024 using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) neuroimaging found higher neural efficiency in the prefrontal cortex during 3D spatial tasks compared to equivalent tasks performed on two-dimensional screens, suggesting that 3D presentation can make complex spatial reasoning genuinely easier for the brain to process.

## 3.2 Isolation and Loss of Shared Context

One of the most consistently cited challenges of remote work is the erosion of shared context — the common understanding of a project, space, or dataset that colleagues naturally develop when working in the same physical environment. Traditional remote collaboration tools such as video conferencing and document sharing struggle to convey spatial or multi-layered information, meaning remote workers may form divergent mental models of the same subject.

Research published in Frontiers in Psychology in 2025 found that virtual platforms have significant potential to replicate many aspects of physical presence and promote more inclusive team dynamics, and that real-time shared interfaces in 3D environments allow team members to brainstorm, edit, and solve problems collectively — meaningfully reducing the isolation that traditional remote work setups bring.

## 3.3 Digital Fatigue and Engagement

Standard flat-screen remote work tools contribute to what has become known as "Zoom fatigue" — the cognitive and emotional exhaustion associated with extended video conferencing. Research by Microsoft has indicated that brain wave patterns associated with stress and overwork are significantly higher during virtual meetings than in-person interactions, driven in part by the unnatural cognitive effort required to read context from a flattened, low-context interface.

Three-dimensional environments offer a richer sensory and spatial context that can reduce this friction. Studies of immersive virtual collaboration environments report an 85% improvement in employee engagement and a 30% increase in productivity, with 75% of organisations actively exploring such technologies to address remote collaboration challenges.

# 4. 3D Applications in Office Environments

Commercial office environments were among the first sectors to experience the productive potential of 3D collaboration tools at scale, particularly as engineering, architecture, product design, and data analytics teams adapted to distributed working arrangements.

## 4.1 Collaborative Design and Product Development

Perhaps the most mature use case for non-VR 3D in office settings is collaborative product and design work. Traditional workflows relying on physical prototypes, 2D drawings, and sequential review processes are poorly suited to geographically distributed teams. Browser-based 3D applications replace these with shared digital models that all stakeholders can access, rotate, annotate, and modify simultaneously regardless of location.

Research into 3D modelling workflows in product design has found that replacing traditional methods with 3D modelling can reduce development turnaround times from weeks to days by enabling virtual testing of variations and eliminating the need for costly physical prototypes. This process not only eliminates ambiguity but also reduces design errors, creating stronger alignment among designers, engineers, marketers, and clients — a particularly significant benefit when those parties work in different locations.

Real-time collaborative 3D applications extend this further by enabling global teams to work on the same 3D scene simultaneously, making changes visible to all participants in real time. This kind of collaboration accelerates development cycles and ensures all parties remain aligned with the latest version of a design — a challenge that has historically required frequent, expensive in-person review sessions.

> **CASE STUDY: Cloud-Based 3D Design Collaboration in Product Teams**
>
> The growth of cloud-based 3D platforms such as Onshape, Autodesk Fusion 360, and web-based viewers built on Three.js and Babylon.js has enabled distributed product design teams to conduct real-time collaborative design reviews without any physical co-location. Teams in architecture, automotive, consumer electronics, and industrial design now routinely use these platforms to share, annotate, and iterate on 3D models from separate continents. Early adopters have reported elimination of costly physical prototype cycles during early design stages and significant reductions in miscommunication-driven rework, both of which are magnified in remote settings where casual desk-side clarifications are unavailable.

## 4.2 3D Data Visualisation for Business Intelligence

Business intelligence and data analytics represent a rapidly growing application area for non-VR 3D. As datasets grow in complexity and interdependency, three-dimensional visualisation can expose patterns, relationships, and anomalies that are genuinely difficult or impossible to perceive in two-dimensional charts and tables.

Research published in 2024 on the impact of interactive data visualisation on business intelligence decision-making explored the use of 3D game engine technology and virtual environments for visualising sustainability and financial data. Using photo-elicitation methods with academics, preparers, and users of sustainability reports, the study found that 3D visualisations enhanced comprehension and generated richer responses than two-dimensional equivalents, with participants identifying new analytical directions and questions that the flat representations had not prompted.

For remote office workers, this matters because data reviews and analytical discussions conducted over video calls rely heavily on the quality and clarity of shared visual material. Three-dimensional dashboards that all participants can navigate interactively — rather than passively viewing a screen-share of a flat chart — transform data meetings from one-way broadcasts into genuinely collaborative analytical sessions.

## 4.3 Virtual Office Environments and Spatial Presence

A more novel but increasingly researched application involves the use of browser-accessible 3D virtual office environments — spatial representations of workplaces in which remote employees can navigate, encounter colleagues, join meetings in contextual spaces, and maintain a sense of physical presence within an organisational environment.

Research published in Frontiers in Psychology (2025) examined four workplace environments: physical presence, videoconferencing, non-immersive virtual 3D, and fully immersive virtual environments. Across 103 participants conducting both collaborative decision-making tasks and information retention tasks, the study found that virtual platforms, including non-immersive 3D, could effectively replicate aspects of physical collaborative dynamics and promote more inclusive team participation — including reducing gender-based participation disparities that persist in physical settings.

A broader review of virtual platforms and their effects on creativity and problem-solving found that immersive and interactive 3D work environments increase employee performance and job satisfaction, and that the metaverse-style spatial collaboration environments enhance the creative performance of virtual teams by providing collaborative and immersive space for idea generation.

# 5. 3D Applications in Government Organisations

Government agencies operate at a unique intersection of complexity, public accountability, and multi-agency coordination. They manage spatial assets (infrastructure, land, borders), serve geographically distributed communities, and must communicate complex policy decisions to diverse audiences. Browser-based 3D applications have found a compelling fit across several government functions.

## 5.1 Urban Digital Twins and Planning

Perhaps the most significant government adoption of non-VR 3D technology is the emergence of Urban Digital Twins — dynamic, browser-accessible three-dimensional replicas of cities that integrate live data from IoT sensors, satellite imagery, GIS databases, and planning systems. These tools allow government planners, engineers, and policymakers to simulate, analyse, and communicate urban conditions from any location.

Urban Digital Twins are reshaping how cities are planned and managed. These virtual city replicas combine data from multiple sources to test urban plans digitally before they are implemented, providing real-time and predictive projections that empower evidence-based decision-making. For remote working planners and policy teams, this means the data and spatial context required for major infrastructure decisions can be accessed, shared, and interrogated collaboratively without requiring co-location in a specialised visualisation room.

> **CASE STUDY: Virtual Singapore — Government Decision-Making at Scale**
>
> Singapore's Virtual Singapore digital twin project, initiated in 2014 and continuously developed since, is used by the government for day-to-day planning and decision-making. It combines detailed 3D models of every building in the city-state with demographic, environmental, and infrastructure data. The platform has been used to plan solar panel deployments across thousands of rooftops, model sea-level rise impacts on coastal districts, and optimise emergency response routing. Government planners across multiple agencies access and contribute to this shared 3D environment, enabling coordinated decision-making without requiring physical meetings or co-location.

> **CASE STUDY: Helsinki 3D+ Platform — Open Government Data in 3D**
>
> Helsinki's 3D+ platform is an open-data digital twin integrating building data from the city's BIM library, terrain models, population data, and climate projections. It has been used to analyse urban heat island effects in specific neighbourhoods and model the cooling impact of different urban greening interventions. Crucially, the platform is publicly accessible via web browser, meaning city planners, developers, and members of the public can access the same 3D environment — enabling distributed, remote engagement with planning decisions that previously required specialist software or in-person presentations.

A systematic review of urban digital twin research published in ScienceDirect in 2025 found a clear trend away from static 3D models toward interactive, collaborative environments, with 75% of reviewed studies facilitating two-way communication between the system and users. The review confirmed that 3D digital twins move beyond simple data visualisation to provide planners with powerful analytical tools for identifying areas requiring improvement, testing interventions, and supporting evidence-based planning — all of which translate to remote working contexts through browser accessibility.

## 5.2 Multi-Agency Coordination and Infrastructure Management

Government operations frequently involve coordination across multiple agencies, departments, and sometimes across national boundaries. Physical infrastructure — road networks, utilities, environmental systems, border facilities — is inherently spatial and benefits from 3D representation. Browser-based 3D platforms allow geographically distributed government teams to share a common operational picture without requiring physical presence at a centralised control facility.

AR and VR remote assistance research has found that 3D tools allow support teams to collaborate remotely and immersively with frontline workers from any geolocation, and that assets can be managed cost-effectively without the travel previously required. While that research encompasses VR systems, the underlying principle — that spatially-aware remote collaboration reduces the need for physical co-location in asset management and technical support — applies equally to browser-based 3D platforms accessible to government field teams and central coordination staff.

The Eurocities network, working with the Barcelona Supercomputing Center, conducted training programmes for European city officials in 2024 specifically focused on implementing Urban Digital Twins in their decision-making processes. Participants emphasised the value of collaborative 3D tools for breaking down silos between city departments and enabling shared understanding across agencies — a particularly salient benefit for hybrid and remote teams.

## 5.3 Public Engagement and Transparency

Government organisations are under increasing pressure to engage citizens meaningfully in planning and policy processes. Three-dimensional visualisation has demonstrated measurable impact in this area. Research examining government data visualisation found that interactive visual formats are preferred by 78% of users over static reports, and that cities implementing interactive dashboards have observed increases of 30–35% in community participation at related meetings and workshops.

Three-dimensional browser-accessible models extend this effect further. When citizens can navigate a 3D model of a proposed development, infrastructure change, or environmental scenario in their own browser — from their home, rather than having to attend an in-person public meeting — participation barriers are significantly reduced. This is not merely a convenience: it represents a structural change in who can engage with government decision-making, with particular benefit for those with mobility constraints, irregular working hours, or caregiving responsibilities that make in-person attendance difficult.

Urban digital twin platforms like Helsinki's open 3D model are explicitly designed with public accessibility in mind: the same 3D environment used by city planners is available in a browser to any resident, enabling genuine dialogue between professional planners and the communities they serve — regardless of where either party is located.

# 6. 3D Applications in Educational Institutions

Education has emerged as one of the most active research areas for 3D application adoption, driven by the disruption of remote learning during the COVID-19 pandemic and sustained by growing evidence that three-dimensional interactive environments meaningfully improve learning outcomes for certain types of content.

## 6.1 Desktop Virtual Learning Environments

Desktop Virtual Learning Environments (DVLEs) — non-headset 3D environments accessed via standard computers — have attracted significant research attention as accessible, scalable alternatives to VR-based educational tools. Unlike VR headsets, DVLEs can be deployed to any student with a standard computer, making them practical at institutional scale.

A peer-reviewed study published in February 2026 (Wu et al., Dalian University) examined the impact of 3D interactive prompts within DVLEs on university students learning physics. Using a quasi-experimental design with eye-tracking and multimodal learning analytics, the study found that students in the 3D prompt condition achieved significantly superior learning outcomes compared to those using conventional two-dimensional prompts, particularly in spatial understanding and dynamic reasoning. Eye-tracking data showed that 3D prompts directed visual attention more effectively and reduced cognitive load — meaning students in the 3D condition were working more efficiently, not just harder.

The study confirmed that embedding 3D interactive prompts in DVLEs effectively directs visual attention, alleviates cognitive burden, and improves learning efficiency, offering valuable implications for the design of immersive educational settings — including those delivered remotely.

## 6.2 STEM Education and Complex Concept Visualisation

Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics education deals extensively with three-dimensional structures, processes, and relationships that are poorly served by two-dimensional diagrams. Browser-accessible 3D tools enable students to interact with molecular structures, anatomical models, physics simulations, and mathematical forms in ways that develop genuine spatial intuition.

A systematic review of immersive technologies in STEM education published in Frontiers in Education (2024) found strong evidence that 3D visualisation tools enhance student engagement and retention by enabling learners to visualise and manipulate 3D models of anatomical structures, mechanical systems, and other complex objects that would otherwise be challenging to understand through two-dimensional representation. The review noted particular benefit for subjects involving three-dimensional geometry, molecular chemistry, biological systems, and engineering design.

Research in neuroimaging for scientific visualisation has demonstrated that BrainBrowser — a browser-based WebGL tool developed for neuroimaging data — allows distributed research teams to access and collaboratively analyse complex 3D brain scan data without requiring specialist software installation, enabling remote collaboration on scientific analysis that previously required co-location with dedicated visualisation equipment. The principle extends to any scientific domain where three-dimensional data visualisation supports collaborative research.

> **CASE STUDY: Rady Children's Hospital — 3D Anatomy for Medical Education**
>
> In June 2024, Rady Children's Hospital in San Diego released its Arc Viewer 3D visualisation software to hospitals across the US. Developed by the hospital's 3D Innovations Lab, the tool enables medical students, trainees, and surgeons to engage with three-dimensional representations of patient anatomy from CT and MRI imaging. The browser-accessible system allows remote learning teams and distributed surgical planning groups to collaboratively examine anatomical structures in 3D — fundamentally changing the educational experience for medical trainees who are not physically co-located with specialist staff.

> **CASE STUDY: BrainBrowser — Collaborative Neuroimaging at Distance**
>
> BrainBrowser, developed for distributed neuroscience research teams, leverages WebGL to enable real-time 3D visualisation of brain imaging datasets in any web browser without plugins. The system allows researchers in different institutions and countries to simultaneously examine and annotate the same 3D neurological data, facilitating a form of collaborative scientific analysis that was previously only possible in specialist facilities. Researchers from multiple universities have used the platform to conduct collaborative analyses across continents, with shared cursor and annotation tools enabling synchronous remote examination of complex 3D datasets.

## 6.3 Engagement, Retention, and Motivation

Beyond specific learning outcomes, 3D educational environments influence broader student engagement and motivation — factors that are particularly important in remote learning contexts where the physical cues, social presence, and informal interactions of a classroom are absent.

Research published in Springer's Technology, Knowledge and Learning (2024) found that mixed reality and 3D remote learning tools provide new opportunities for students and teachers from different places to be part of shared, engaging learning experiences. Students and teachers from different parts of the world can convene for learning activities and knowledge exchange, with the shared 3D environment creating a sense of common context that video conferencing alone cannot replicate.

A broader review of educational technology in higher education published in 2024 concluded that educational technology including 3D environments, simulations, and virtual reality has potential for improving learning outcomes, motivation, engagement, and pass rates. The review noted that 3D tools enable students to be involved in teaching activities that were previously inconceivable — interactive simulations, collaborative model building, and virtual fieldwork that extend the educational experience beyond what is possible in a conventional remote classroom.

Research on collaborative learning with 3D visual structures found that shared 3D workspaces could achieve better user performance and engagement levels than those relying on non-spatial digital tools, particularly for tasks involving abstract or complex spatial concepts — exactly the type of content that most benefits from remote educational delivery in science and technology disciplines.

# 7. Cross-Sector Benefits and Enabling Factors

Across offices, government organisations, and educational institutions, several consistent benefits and enabling conditions emerge from the evidence.

## 7.1 Accessibility as a Strategic Advantage

The defining advantage of browser-based 3D over headset-based VR in organisational contexts is accessibility. Because DVLEs and browser 3D applications require only a web browser and a standard computer, they can be deployed to entire workforces without hardware procurement, IT configuration, or device management overhead. This is particularly significant for government agencies and educational institutions operating under budget constraints and strict procurement processes.

WebXR standards and the transition from WebGL to WebGPU are further reducing technical barriers. As noted by leading web technology researchers, the widespread adoption of WebGPU by major browsers will drastically enhance graphics performance, making complex real-time rendering widely accessible — without any action required from end-users beyond keeping their browser updated.

## 7.2 Reduction in Travel and Physical Meeting Costs

For all three sectors examined, 3D remote collaboration tools reduce the need for in-person meetings, site visits, and physical reviews. AR and VR remote assistance research has established that operational issues can be resolved quickly and assets managed cost-effectively when support teams can collaborate remotely using 3D visualisation. For government agencies managing distributed infrastructure or educational institutions conducting remote site assessments, this translates to meaningful cost savings and reduced carbon footprint.

## 7.3 Inclusive Participation

Three-dimensional remote environments have shown particular potential for inclusive participation. Research has found that virtual 3D environments can reduce gender-based participation disparities that persist in physical meetings. For government public engagement and educational contexts, browser-accessible 3D tools remove the geographic, mobility, and time-based barriers to participation that limit who can engage with in-person processes.

## 7.4 Real-Time Synchronisation of Shared Understanding

A recurring theme across all three sectors is the value of shared, synchronised visual context. When all participants in a distributed team navigate the same 3D environment in real time, they build common understanding more efficiently than is possible through sequential document review or flat-screen presentations. This shared context is fundamental to effective collaboration and decision-making, and it is precisely what physical co-location provides — and what flat-screen remote tools struggle to replicate.

# 8. Challenges, Limitations, and Considerations

While the evidence for the value of non-VR 3D applications in remote workplaces is substantial, a complete picture requires acknowledging important challenges and limitations.

## 8.1 Cognitive Ergonomics and Fatigue

Research examining virtual platform design has found that key differences persist in cognitive load, comfort, and engagement — especially in immersive settings. Poorly designed 3D interfaces can increase rather than reduce cognitive load, and users may experience fatigue from navigation-heavy 3D environments in extended sessions. The design of virtual environments must carefully balance immersive features with user comfort and cognitive ergonomics.

For organisations considering 3D tool adoption, this means that interface design quality matters enormously. A well-designed browser-based 3D application that provides clear navigation cues, appropriate levels of environmental complexity, and intuitive interaction patterns will deliver different outcomes than a poorly implemented one.

## 8.2 Task Suitability

Not all tasks benefit equally from 3D representation. Research suggests that the benefits of 3D interfaces are most pronounced for tasks involving spatial reasoning, multi-layered data, collaborative design, and complex system comprehension. For straightforward text-based tasks, sequential processes, or one-dimensional data, 3D environments may add unnecessary complexity without corresponding benefit.

Effective adoption requires matching the level of immersion to task type — using 3D environments for active collaboration and spatial tasks while opting for simpler interfaces in administrative or sequential workflows.

## 8.3 Technical Requirements and Equity

While browser-based 3D is far more accessible than headset-based VR, it still places demands on hardware and network infrastructure. Complex 3D scenes may perform poorly on older computers or over slow internet connections. In educational and government contexts where device and connectivity equity is a policy concern, these requirements must be considered in deployment planning.

Performance optimisation — including progressive loading, level-of-detail management, and adaptive quality settings — can mitigate many of these concerns, but implementation quality remains an important factor.

## 8.4 Change Management and Training

Introducing 3D interfaces to workforces accustomed to flat-screen tools involves organisational change. Staff training, workflow redesign, and cultural adjustment are necessary components of successful adoption. Research on digital twin implementation in government has noted the importance of building institutional capacity alongside technical deployment, with training and peer learning programmes playing a significant role in sustainable adoption.

## 8.5 Research Maturity

While the evidence base for 3D collaboration tools is growing rapidly, it is not yet as mature or voluminous as research on conventional productivity software. Many studies involve relatively small participant groups, specific subject domains, or short-term outcome measures. Longitudinal research on the long-term productivity and wellbeing impacts of browser-based 3D environments in organisational settings remains limited, and organisations should treat current evidence as promising but still developing.

# 9. Recommendations for Adoption

Based on the evidence reviewed in this paper, the following recommendations are offered for organisations in office, government, and educational contexts considering adoption of non-VR 3D applications for remote workplaces.

## 9.1 Start with High-Value Use Cases

Prioritise deployment in areas where the gap between 2D representation and 3D understanding is most consequential: architectural and space planning, geographic data analysis, product and infrastructure design review, complex scientific visualisation, and spatial skills education. These contexts offer the clearest return on investment and the most compelling demonstration of value to stakeholders.

## 9.2 Leverage Existing Browser Infrastructure

Because WebGL is universally supported in modern browsers, no organisation needs to invest in specialist hardware to begin experimenting with browser-based 3D. Begin by piloting available platforms — whether commercial tools like Onshape, CesiumJS-based geographic dashboards, or open-source WebGL frameworks — before investing in bespoke development.

## 9.3 Design for Collaboration First

The most significant value of 3D tools in remote workplaces comes from shared, synchronised access. Prioritise platforms and tools that support simultaneous multi-user access, real-time annotation, shared camera views, and integration with existing communication tools. A sophisticated 3D model that only one person can view at a time delivers only a fraction of the potential value.

## 9.4 Invest in Interface Quality

The evidence is clear that design quality matters. A well-designed 3D interface reduces cognitive load and improves comprehension; a poorly designed one does the opposite. Allocate appropriate resource to UX design, user testing, and iterative refinement, particularly for tools deployed to non-specialist audiences in government or educational contexts.

## 9.5 Build Evaluation into Deployment

Given the still-developing nature of the evidence base, organisations that adopt 3D remote collaboration tools have an opportunity to contribute to the knowledge base by systematically evaluating outcomes: productivity metrics, error rates, decision-making speed, staff satisfaction, student learning outcomes, and public engagement levels. This data strengthens the internal business case and contributes to sector-wide understanding.

## 9.6 Approach Change Management Proactively

Technical deployment without organisational preparation is unlikely to succeed. Designate 3D tool champions within teams, provide adequate training, create structured workflows that incorporate 3D review sessions, and communicate clearly about the purpose and expected benefits of the new tools. In educational institutions, involve academic staff in design decisions to ensure pedagogical alignment.

# 10. Conclusion

The evidence reviewed in this paper makes a compelling case for the value of non-VR, browser-based 3D applications in remote workplace environments across offices, government organisations, and educational institutions. The case rests on several converging lines of evidence.

Cognitively, three-dimensional visual representation reduces the mental effort required for spatial reasoning tasks and enhances comprehension of complex, multi-layered information — precisely the type of information that organisations most need to share and discuss effectively in distributed settings. Neuroimaging research confirms genuine differences in brain activity during 3D versus 2D spatial tasks, with 3D showing higher neural efficiency.

Practically, the technology is now accessible without specialist hardware. Browser-based 3D, powered by WebGL and the emerging WebGPU standard, runs on any modern desktop computer with no installation, making it deployable to entire workforces at minimal marginal cost. This distinguishes it fundamentally from headset-based VR as a scalable organisational tool.

Empirically, case studies from governments including Singapore, Helsinki, and multiple European cities demonstrate that 3D digital environments are already embedded in real decision-making processes, with measurable impacts on planning quality and cross-agency coordination. Educational research from China, the United States, and Europe consistently finds improved learning outcomes, engagement, and retention when 3D environments are used for spatially complex subject matter. Office productivity research points to meaningful gains in design iteration speed, reduction of costly physical prototyping, and improvement in distributed team alignment.

The market trajectory reinforces these findings: the 3D visualisation software market is projected to grow from USD 30 billion in 2025 to USD 108 billion by 2035, with cloud-based remote collaboration driving much of that growth. The visual collaboration platform market is growing even faster, at 22.9% annually, precisely because organisations have recognised that flat-screen remote tools leave value unrealised.

Important caveats remain. Interface design quality matters enormously — poorly designed 3D environments increase rather than reduce cognitive burden. Task matching is critical — not everything benefits from 3D representation. And the evidence base, while growing rapidly, has not yet achieved the depth and longitudinal scope that would support high-confidence claims about every application context.

Nevertheless, the weight of current evidence supports a clear conclusion: for organisations operating with distributed or remote teams — particularly where spatial information, complex data, collaborative design, or spatial learning are central to the work — investment in high-quality, browser-accessible 3D applications represents a strategic opportunity to close the gap between the richness of physical co-location and the practical reality of remote work. The tools are here, the research is supportive, and the technology is accessible. The question for organisations is not whether to engage with 3D remote collaboration, but where to begin.
